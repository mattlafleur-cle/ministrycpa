Pastor as 1099 Contractor: Why Your Ministry Is at Risk

By Matt LaFleur, CPA

Somewhere along the way, a lot of churches picked up the idea that pastors are simply self-employed. It is an understandable conclusion, and it is wrong often enough to be worth a closer look. Ministers really do have an unusual tax status. It is just not the one most people assume.

Two different tests, two different answers

For federal income tax withholding, a minister who serves a congregation the way an employee does, called to the role, supervised by the church, given duties and a schedule the church controls, is treated as a common-law employee. That means a W-2, not a 1099-NEC, for ministerial services performed for the employing church.

For Social Security and Medicare taxes, the law goes the other way. Ministers performing ministerial duties are treated as self-employed, always, regardless of their W-2 status for income tax purposes. This is the dual tax status that makes ministers genuinely different from almost every other kind of employee, and it is covered directly in IRS Publication 517.

Put together: a pastor can be a common-law employee who receives a W-2, with no federal income tax withheld unless they voluntarily request it, while still owing self-employment tax on their ministerial earnings through Schedule SE instead of having FICA split with the church. Two different rules, two different purposes, and it is easy to see how "self-employed for Social Security" gets misread as "self-employed, full stop, so issue a 1099."

What is actually at risk

Misclassifying a pastor as a 1099 contractor is not a paperwork technicality. A few concrete consequences:

  • Reclassification exposure. If the IRS determines the pastor was a common-law employee, the church can face back withholding assessments and penalties for the years it was handled incorrectly.
  • Lost flexibility on withholding. A W-2 minister can voluntarily elect to have the church withhold enough to cover both income tax and their anticipated self-employment tax, avoiding quarterly estimated payments entirely. A 1099 arrangement forfeits that option.
  • Benefit and retirement plan complications. Clergy-specific retirement plans and certain housing allowance treatments generally assume an employee relationship. A 1099 classification can create friction with plans built around that assumption.
  • Messier records exactly where you need them cleanest. Housing allowance reporting, benevolence, and reimbursements all get harder to document correctly once the underlying employment classification is wrong.

How to tell which one applies

The test is about control and relationship, not title. A pastor the church calls to the role, assigns duties to, sets a schedule for, and can remove from the position is almost always a common-law employee. A guest speaker paid a one-time honorarium for a single event is a different situation entirely, and a 1099 may well be appropriate there. The mistake is applying the contractor logic to the ongoing, called role of a senior or associate pastor.

If your pastor has a set role, a set schedule, and answers to your board the way a staff member does, the paperwork should say employee, even though the tax return will still look like a self-employed person's in places.

If your ministry currently issues a 1099-NEC for a pastor's ongoing ministerial role, this is worth revisiting before the next filing season, while there is still time to correct it cleanly.

— Matt LaFleur, CPA
