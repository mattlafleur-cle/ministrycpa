Clergy Taxes

Clergy taxes, explained

The tax rules for pastors and ministers are genuinely different from a typical employee's. Here's what actually applies, in plain English.

By Matt LaFleur, CPA

Why clergy taxes are different

Most pastors and ministers have what's called "dual tax status": they're treated as an employee for federal income tax purposes, but as self-employed for Social Security and Medicare purposes. No other common job works this way, which is exactly why so many churches and even some tax preparers get it wrong (IRS, Members of the clergy).

The housing allowance

A minister's housing allowance, or the value of a church-provided parsonage, can be excluded from federal income tax if the church designates it in advance, in writing, and the minister stays within three specific limits. It's still included in earnings for self-employment tax, which is the most common surprise at tax time.

Dual status and SECA

Because a minister is self-employed for Social Security and Medicare purposes, a church should not withhold FICA from a minister's pay the way it would for any other employee. Instead, the minister pays self-employment tax (SECA) directly, on Schedule SE, covering both their wages and their housing allowance. A church that withholds FICA from a pastor's paycheck is making one of the most common clergy-payroll mistakes there is (IRS Publication 517).

Opting out of Social Security (Form 4361)

A minister who is conscientiously opposed, on religious grounds, to receiving public insurance benefits for services as a minister (not opposed for economic reasons) may be able to apply for an exemption from self-employment tax using Form 4361. There are strict deadlines tied to when a minister first has ministerial earnings, and once an exemption is approved, it generally cannot be revoked. This is a significant, permanent decision, not a routine box to check, and it deserves a real conversation before filing.

Is a minister an employee or self-employed? (W-2 vs. 1099)

For income tax withholding purposes, most pastors serving a congregation are common-law employees and should receive a W-2, not a 1099. Issuing a 1099 to a pastor who is actually a common-law employee is a real compliance risk for the church, not just a paperwork preference.
