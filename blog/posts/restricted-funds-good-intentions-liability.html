Restricted Funds: When Good Intentions Become Liability

By Matt LaFleur, CPA

A donor gives $10,000 for the youth mission trip. A month later, payroll is short $4,000. Someone quietly moves the money over, fully intending to put it back before anyone notices. Nobody involved thinks of this as dishonest. It usually is not meant that way. It is also one of the more serious mistakes a ministry can make with money that was never fully its own to begin with.

What "restricted" actually means

When a donor designates a gift for a specific purpose, that designation is not just a bookkeeping label. It functions as a condition attached to the gift. Once your ministry accepts a restricted donation, it takes on a legal and fiduciary obligation to spend that money only on what the donor specified, not on whatever is most urgent that week.

Current nonprofit accounting standards, following ASU 2016-14, classify these as net assets with donor restrictions and net assets without donor restrictions. Many treasurers still use the older language, temporarily and permanently restricted funds, which is fine informally, but it is worth knowing your financial statements should reflect the current terms.

Why "we'll pay it back" does not make it fine

Intent to repay does not change what happened: restricted money was spent on something other than its restriction, even temporarily. A few real consequences that follow from that:

  • It can violate state law. Many states treat charitable solicitations and the funds they raise as subject to trust-like obligations, not just internal policy.
  • It damages donor trust in a way that is hard to undo. A donor who learns their gift was redirected, even briefly and even if it was fully restored, understandably questions whether to give restricted gifts again.
  • It is a classic audit and Form 990 red flag. Reviewers know to look for restricted balances that dip below what the organization has actually received and not yet spent.
  • In repeated or significant cases, it can raise real 501(c)(3) compliance questions, not just an internal control footnote.

What proper stewardship looks like

You do not need a separate bank account for every restricted gift, though some ministries choose to for their largest funds. What you do need:

  • Fund-level tracking in your general ledger, so a restricted balance is always visible and can never be spent below what is actually owed to that purpose
  • A rule that changing how a restricted gift gets used requires formal board action, and in most cases the donor's written consent. A ministry cannot simply decide on its own to redirect a gift a donor restricted
  • A short, written gift acceptance policy that defines what counts as a restriction and spells out how a request to change one is handled, so the answer does not depend on whoever happens to be in the office that day
The failure here is almost never dishonesty. It is cash flow pressure meeting the absence of a clear boundary. The fix is building the boundary before the pressure shows up, not while it is already happening.

If your ministry has ever moved money between funds "temporarily," that is worth a real conversation with your board, not a one-time fix and moving on.

— Matt LaFleur, CPA
